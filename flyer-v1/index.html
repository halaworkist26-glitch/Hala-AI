The idea

Every company runs two operations.

One does the work your customers pay for. The other moves the information about that work.

The visible operationMoving freight, making products, trading goods, delivering projects.
The information operationUpdates, documents, customer questions, records, reports.

The second operation lives in inboxes, spreadsheets, paper files and people's memories. It grows with the business, and it is often handled by hand.

That second operation is where AI gets interesting.

The gap

A strong business, with no technical department.

Many established companies have real operational activity and experienced people, but no internal team to evaluate or build AI. Hiring one is a large decision to make before you know what AI could do for you.

So the questions stay open:

  • Where would AI actually be useful for us?
  • Would it work with the systems we already have?
  • What would it take to find out?

You can explore these questions before making any commitment, and without hiring anyone. The rest of this guide shows how.

What AI can and cannot do

AI supports your people. The decisions stay with you.

What AI can support

  • Finding and summarizing company information
  • Drafting routine updates and replies
  • Sorting and checking document data
  • Bringing numbers together into reports
  • Automating suitable repetitive steps

What stays with your people

  • Decisions and approvals
  • Customer and supplier relationships
  • Judgment on unusual cases
  • Priorities and strategy

AI can make mistakes, so important outputs should be reviewed by a person. A well-designed system is built with that review in mind.

One example

A single customer question, start to finish.

Illustrative example, not a client case

"Where is my shipment, and are the customs documents complete?"

Often today: someone checks the tracking system, searches old emails for the documents, asks a colleague, then writes a reply.

With a custom assistant, it could look like this:

  • It reads the message and finds the shipment in your records.
  • It checks which documents are on file and which look missing.
  • It drafts a reply for your employee.
  • Your employee reviews it, edits it and sends it.

Your options

Three ways to bring AI into a company.

Ready-made software

Quick to try, and you adapt your process to the product. A good fit when a standard tool already solves the problem.

An internal AI team

Full in-house control, with the hiring and management that comes with it. A good fit when AI is central to your long-term plans.

An independent engineer

Designed around your process, starting small, with no department to build. A good fit when you want to explore a specific need before a larger commitment.

If a ready-made tool is the better answer for you, that is worth knowing early, and I will say so.

Common questions

Questions you may already have.

Do we need technical staff?

No. You know your operation and I handle the technical side. Someone who knows the daily work should be available for questions.

Will this replace our employees?

That is not the aim. The aim is to support them and reduce repetitive steps.

Will it work with our current systems?

Often, but it depends on the systems. Checking this is part of the first stage.

What about our data?

Where data is stored, which AI services are used and who can access what are agreed before anything is built.

How long does it take?

It depends on the scope. You would get an estimate once the first step is defined.

What should we prepare?

Nothing for the first conversation. Just bring the process that frustrates you most.
